The setup: two AIs, two jobs

This is the key to everything else. Never make one do the other's work.

The worker

{{CODING_AI}}

Already connected to the {{REPO}} repository. It writes and runs the code. It does not decide the method, and it does not decide the scope.

The project manager

{{COPILOT_NAME}}

{{STEERING_AI}}, which you are going to set up. It frames each step, designs, reviews what {{CODING_AI}} produces and holds the method. It never touches the code.

{{COPILOT_NAME}} wears two hats

Guarantor of the solution — it is your architect, your quality control and the keeper of the method. It is the one that refuses to move on while a step is unfinished. Mentor — it brings you up to speed on what you need to understand to stay in charge. It offers; it never forces a lesson on you that you did not want.

If it skips that introduction, it has lost the thread: ask for it again.

{{COPILOT_NAME}} cannot see the repository. That is deliberate. An AI left believing it has access to the code will invent its contents with great confidence. When it needs a file, it asks you: you copy it across or send it over. You are the bridge.

How a need becomes a feature

It is not your job to write what the application should do. You describe your work; {{COPILOT_NAME}} turns it into an item.

  1. You describe

    How things go today: who does what, when, with what, and what actually gets stuck. Your work, not the screen you imagine. {{COPILOT_NAME}} asks you simple questions, one at a time, in plain language. If it sends you ten at once, tell it so: it is the one going about it wrongly.

  2. It plays it back, you confirm

    It tells you what it understood, in its own words, and waits for your agreement before writing anything. This is the cheapest moment to correct things: a misunderstood need costs ten times more once it is in code.

  3. It writes the item

    One item per need: who uses it, what they do, what it gives them, and the list of what must be visible on screen to call it done. With a number and a priority. Anything that cannot be shown is not an item.

  4. You approve, and it gets filed

    The approved item joins the repository, and only then. Nothing gets built before that.

    You approve when: you can say the item back in your own words. If you cannot, it is not right: we go over it again.

You write none of it yourself. You are the expert in your own work; turning that work into items is {{COPILOT_NAME}}'s job. If it asks you to write your requirements for it, it has lost the thread: send it back to this page.

Where things land, and which one is right

Three places, three uses. Only one is authoritative: if two disagree, you know which to believe.

PlaceWhat goes in itAuthoritative?
The repository — {{SPEC_DIR}} The need items and the specification, as files. Yes — that is the reference
{{TRACKING_BOARD}} Progress: one card per item, drawn from the files. No — it gets rebuilt
{{WIKI}} What a human reads outside the code: decisions and their reasons, procedures, how-tos. For its own subject only

A card is never filled in before the file. If a {{TRACKING_BOARD}} card says something different from the repository file, the file is right: the card gets rebuilt. And a specification never gets copied into {{WIKI}} — two versions of the same text always end up contradicting each other, and nobody knows which to read any more.

The repository is private, and it must stay that way. Making it public is {{COACH}}'s decision, never a setting ticked in passing: it holds {{ORGANISATION}}'s workings and data. If you come across that button in the settings, leave it alone.

And if it gets stuck

  • {{COPILOT_NAME}} charges ahead without explaining? Tell it: "remind me of the frame of the step before moving on". It is in its instructions, it has to.
  • It talks about a file it does not have? It cannot see the repository. Copy the file across — and be wary if it describes the contents of something you never gave it.
  • You do not understand a word? Ask. It wears a mentor's hat, that is exactly its role — and a word not understood today is a decision imposed on you tomorrow.
  • Someone suggests adding "just one small thing"? Note it down and refuse it for now. One project at a time.
  • {{COPILOT_NAME}} asks you to write your requirements yourself? That is the opposite of its role. Tell it again to ask you simple questions, one at a time, about your work, and to write the items itself afterwards.
  • An access is blocking you? Repository or hosting, that is for {{COACH}}: they alone can invite, and they do it once. Ask {{COPILOT_NAME}} to write the message for you: finding the right words is not your job.
  • Waiting on a reply from {{COACH}}? Ask {{COPILOT_NAME}} what you can get on with meanwhile. There is nearly always something — and if it leaves you at a standstill with nothing to offer, insist.
  • A {{TRACKING_BOARD}} card says something different from the repository file? The file is right. Have the card rebuilt, never the other way round.
